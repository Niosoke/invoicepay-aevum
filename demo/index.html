InvoicePay

Stablecoin invoices on the QDay Aevum testnet (chain 44005). It works like this: the payer approves a purchase order on chain first, saying which merchant may bill it, what the total cap is, and when the approval expires. The merchant then bills against that approval, in full or in parts, and the contract turns away anything beyond the approved total. Every invoice names the milestone it bills, and a milestone can be billed once per purchase order. Submit the same milestone again and the contract rejects it, even if budget is still left. The next installment for the same amount goes through, because it names a different milestone. When the named payer pays, the money goes straight to the merchant. The contract never holds funds.

How this demo works

  1. Connect a wallet. The demo adds the QDay Aevum testnet for you.
  2. Acting as the payer, approve a purchase order. You name the merchant, the total cap, and an expiry.
  3. Switch to the merchant account in your wallet and create an invoice against that purchase order. Name the milestone it bills.
  4. Or run the guided check below. It approves a purchase order, bills two equal installments, shows a resubmitted milestone being rejected, and pays both.
  5. Back as the payer, open your invoice view. Check each invoice and its milestone against your approval, pay, and copy the settlement proof for your records.

You need testnet tQDAY for gas and tUSD8 to pay with. Both are free from the faucet at faucet.qday.info. This is a testnet, so nothing here has real value.

1. Connect

The contract address below points at the live deployment. Leave it as it is unless you deployed your own copy.

2. Payer: approve a purchase order

Here you are the payer, and this step comes before any billing. Approve the purchase order and name the one merchant allowed to bill it, the total cap in tUSD8, and how many days the approval stays open for new invoices. The purchase order text is hashed before it goes on chain, so the plain text never appears there. Invoices can settle the cap in parts, but never beyond it. While an approval is still unused you can replace it by approving again. Once an invoice reserves against it, its terms are locked.

3. Merchant: create an invoice

Now you are the merchant. The payer has to approve this purchase order for your address first (step 2). Fill in the payer, the amount, a due date, a memo, the purchase order this invoice settles, and the milestone it bills. The amount can be part of the approved total, say one delivery out of several. The milestone names the piece of work, for example "PO 3001 milestone 1". Each milestone can be billed once per purchase order, so a resubmitted bill gets rejected while the next installment goes through. The contract also rejects the invoice when there is no approval, when the approval names another merchant, when it has expired, when the milestone was already billed, or when the amount would push the total past the approved cap.

Careful with the memo: it is public. It sits on chain and goes out in the invoice event, so keep it fictional or generic. The purchase order reference and the milestone reference are safer. Only their hashes go on chain, never the plain text.

Guided check: two equal installments settle, a resubmitted milestone is rejected

This runs the milestone promise of InvoicePay in five steps, using its own purchase order reference below. Pick a fresh purchase order text (for example PO 4001) so it does not collide with an approval you already made. Steps A and E run as the payer, steps B, C and D as the merchant, so you will switch accounts in your wallet along the way. Both installments carry the same amount on purpose. The milestone each one names is what tells them apart. The memos in this check are neutral labels, also on purpose: a memo is public on chain, while the purchase order and milestone references are stored only as hashes.

Step A. As the payer, approve this purchase order for the merchant above, with the approved total as the cap.

Step B. Switch to the merchant account and create the first installment invoice. It names milestone 1 of this purchase order.

Step C. Still as the merchant, create the second installment for the same amount, this time naming milestone 2. Same amount, different milestone. This one is legitimate and the contract should accept it.

Step D. Still as the merchant, try billing milestone 1 again for the same amount. Budget still remains under the cap, but the milestone is already claimed, so the contract should reject it. A rejection here means the demo is working.

Step E. Switch back to the payer account and pay both installments. Each settles once, and both milestones stay locked afterwards. This step is safe to resume. If you reload the page after paying the first installment, it finds both installment invoices again, pays only the one still open, and reads both statuses back before it reports the outcome. A confirmed payment always shows its transaction hash and proof first, even if a later status reload fails.

4. Payer: invoices addressed to you

Switch to the payer account, then load your invoices. The list comes from InvoiceCreated events filtered by your payer address, so the demo reads only your invoices instead of scanning the whole contract history. If the event query is unavailable it falls back to a bounded scan of the most recent invoices and says so. You can also look up one invoice directly by id. For each invoice the demo reads your purchase order approval and the milestone claim from the contract, so you can check the merchant, the amount, the milestone it bills, and how much of the approved total is already settled before you pay. After payment you get a settlement proof to copy into your records. The proof appears as soon as the payment receipt succeeds and stays on screen even if a later status reload fails.

5. Invoices on chain

Read live from the contract. Refresh after creating or paying an invoice. The PO column shows a short form of the purchase order hash bound to each invoice, and the Milestone column does the same for the milestone hash it bills.
